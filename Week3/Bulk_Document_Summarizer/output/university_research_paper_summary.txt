This study investigates whether spaced repetition scheduling improves the long-term retention of course material among first-year undergraduate biology students compared to traditional massed practice. Utilizing a randomized controlled design with 214 participants over two semesters, researchers evaluated student performance through unannounced assessments administered at one, four, and twelve weeks after instruction. Both groups maintained identical study time targets, but the experimental group utilized an application with expanding review intervals while the control group studied in a single weekly block.

Results revealed that while the control group performed slightly better at the one-week mark, the spaced-repetition group achieved significantly higher scores by week four and an even wider margin by week twelve. The retention advantage was particularly pronounced for terminology-heavy subjects like cellular respiration and taxonomy, alongside smaller yet significant improvements in conceptual short-answer questions. Additionally, participants in the experimental group reported lower exam anxiety and higher confidence throughout the course of the semester.

Ultimately, the findings support integrating spaced-repetition tools into undergraduate science instruction to enhance cumulative knowledge and long-term retention without requiring additional study time. Although the study was limited to a single institution and subject area, the authors suggest that universities embed spacing-based tools into learning management systems. Future research is needed to determine whether these benefits extend to other academic disciplines such as chemistry, physics, and non-STEM subjects.
